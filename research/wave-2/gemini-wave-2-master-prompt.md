## 5. Research process

### Stage 1 — Establish the selection brief

Synthesize the three documents into eligibility/track constraints, selection priorities, important prior art, unresolved facts, and solo-build assumptions. Identify what must be checked before making a confident recommendation.

### Stage 2 — Search broadly before selecting

Develop and screen approximately **20–30 materially different candidate directions** across the four tracks. Start from documented problems and user actions, rather than attaching AI/blockchain to a fashionable app category.

Explore consumer, household, community, and small-organization settings. A business or operator can participate, but an ordinary person's benefit and entry point must be clear. Do not prematurely anchor on one appealing idea or generate six variants of the same ledger, dashboard, scanner, marketplace, or reward system.

Use Wave 1 winners to understand occupied territory and effective demonstrations. A winner establishes recognition, not necessarily deployed impact, novelty today, or why it won. Verify the exact award if introducing additional winners; distinguish winners, finalists, sponsor prizes, and unawarded entries.

### Stage 3 — Investigate serious contenders

For each serious contender, research:

- Evidence that the problem exists and matters to a specific user.
- At least **two close existing alternatives**, including current commercial/open-source products where available. Search the actual function, not only the proposed project name or the phrase “AI blockchain.”
- The exact improvement proposed, and whether public evidence supports its absence or merely leaves it unknown.
- Representative data/API availability, fields, geography, resolution, licensing, access/authentication, costs, and dependencies.
- What one person can actually demonstrate within the remaining time.
- The environmental measurement method and what physical facts remain unverified.
- The strongest objection under each judging criterion.

### Stage 4 — Challenge and narrow

Try to disprove each leading candidate's novelty, data readiness, AI usefulness, blockchain justification, impact assumptions, and adoption story. Explain why the best counterargument does or does not defeat it.

Narrow to **exactly six distinct final candidates**. If a candidate's central mechanism fails, replace or materially rescope it. Do not preserve it merely to fill a track or maintain an attractive pitch.

If public evidence cannot support six exceptionally strong ideas, still present the six best-supported candidates, but distinguish stronger contenders from conditional backups. Do not manufacture confidence or hide a fatal dependency to fill the list.

### Stage 5 — Rank with evidence

Give a clear overall ranking and one provisional recommendation. Explain the trade-offs and the specific new evidence that would change the ordering. Compare balanced, impact-focused, execution-focused, and innovation-focused priorities qualitatively, or recompute any explicitly labeled internal weighted scores correctly. Do not reuse an equal-weight total as though it were a weighted result.

## 6. Evidence and technical honesty requirements

Use primary sources wherever possible: official organizer pages, university/IEEE profiles, original papers, official product documentation, repositories, dataset cards, government sources, and direct API documentation.

- Cite material factual claims near the claim with direct working links and identify the research/access date.
- Distinguish **verified fact, provider-advertised capability, inference, untested assumption, and proposed design**.
- Do not claim worldwide uniqueness from a short search. State the closest alternatives and precise differentiation hypothesis.
- Do not claim an API/dataset was tested unless you actually accessed a response/file. Documentation read, sample inspected, full data parsed, and model evaluated are different evidence levels. If execution is unavailable, say so.
- A source's existence does not establish suitable training labels, representative inputs, licensing, or production access. Name missing inputs explicitly.
- Blockchain can preserve statements and enforce enrolled rules; it cannot independently establish physical truth, honest identity, authentic sensors, a completed repair, actual recycling, or causal emissions reductions.
- AI can extract, estimate, recommend, or flag inconsistencies within its evaluated scope. Do not make it an automatic certifier of image authenticity, material recovery, safety, regulatory compliance, or real-world impact.
- Compare the blockchain design with a signed database/transparency log. Explain which independent parties need common state and what a contract actually enforces.
- Keep averaged, forecast, modeled, metered, self-reported, and simulated values separate. Local grid intensity is not automatically marginal avoided emissions; a monthly total does not reveal interval usage; lower impact per use is not automatically lower total emissions.
- Separate location-based physical electricity accounting from contractual renewable attributes. Do not turn one into a verdict about the other.
- Do not convert registrations, photos, tokens, transactions, donations, repairs, or tracked items into CO₂ avoided without a defensible counterfactual and method.
- Distinguish globally recognized problems from measured project outcomes. Scaling estimates need explicit adoption, displacement, persistence, and operating assumptions.
- Do not assume unavailable hardware, operator partnerships, utility integrations, verified issuers, or paid data. A disclosed replay/proof of concept is acceptable, but cannot be presented as live physical deployment.
- Do not use speculative tokens, unfunded rewards, or future carbon-credit markets to conceal a missing user incentive.

### B. Six detailed project cards

For **each** idea include:

1. **Plain-language problem and pitch:** a memorable 30–60 second explanation, a specific affected user, and sourced problem evidence.
2. **Track and user action:** exact fit, initial adoption setting, what the ordinary person does, and how broader access could work.

5. **AI mechanism:** one primary bounded task, its inputs/outputs, why it helps, a meaningful simple baseline, evaluation data/metrics, uncertainty, and fallback. Separate actual results from proposed acceptance tests.
6. **Blockchain mechanism:** independent actors, conflicting interests, exact state/rule/invariant, on-chain versus private data, identities/oracles, attacks/collusion limits, and comparison with a signed database.
7. **Data-readiness table:** exact sources/URLs, relevant fields, units/geography/resolution, licensing/authentication/cost status, what you actually inspected, missing inputs, and fallback.

10. **3–5 minute demo:** one relatable journey, real software/data versus labeled fixtures, meaningful AI output, genuine ledger behavior, one adversarial/failure case, and the concluding evidence.
11. **Rubric and panel scrutiny:** strengths/weaknesses under all five criteria, plus likely technical questions grounded in verified expertise rather than predicted preferences.
12. **Fatal assumption and first test:** what could make the idea fail, the smallest discriminating feasibility test, a bounded fallback, and when to reject it.
